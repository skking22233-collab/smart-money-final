Why Stock Analysis Matters Warren Buffett the investor shares a famous rule: I never invest in a business if I do not understand the business. Stock analysis helps you understand the stock market. Stock analysis shows you where the prices are moving. I need to understand what the company does. I need to understand how the company makes money. Fundamental Analysis (The Business Side) When I evaluate a company I look at the company's health the company's earnings the company's growth. The company's value. I then ask myself: Is this a company to own? 2. Technical Analysis (The Price Side) I look at the stock price patterns the trends and the trading volume. The stock price patterns the trends and the trading volume analysis asks the question: Is this a time to buy? Best approach: Use analysis to pick what to buy. Use analysis to decide when to buy. Part 1: Fundamental Analysis (Step-by-Step) Step 1: Understand the Business Before looking at any numbers, answer these questions: I want to know what the company does. I want to know how it makes the money. Who are its customers? What industry is it in? Who are its competitors? I am curious, about the product. Does the product have an advantage (moat)? A competitive moat protects the company from competitors. I see a moat in the examples : Brand power (Apple, Nike, Coca-Cola) Network effects (Facebook, Visa) Cost advantage (Walmart, Amazon) Patents/Technology (Pharmaceutical companies) Switching costs (Microsoft, Adobe) Step 2: Analyze Key Financial Statements I read a public company report. A public company publishes three statements: 1. Income Statement (Profit & Loss) Shows revenue, expenses, and profit over a period. Revenue → I am looking at revenue. Is the revenue growing year, over year? Net Income → Is the company profitable? Profit Margin → How much profit per dollar of revenue? 2. Balance Sheet I can see what the company owns the assets and what the company owes the liabilities. Total Assets vs Total Liabilities → Is the company financially stable? Debt levels → Too much debt is risky Cash on hand → I wonder if cash, on hand can survive times? 3. Cash Flow Statement I see cash moving in and out. The screen shows actual cash coming in and cash going out each day. Operating cash flow → Does the core business generate cash? Free cash flow is the cash left after the company pays its expenses and makes its investments. I think free cash flow is what I look at when I check a business. Part 2: Technical Analysis (Basics) Technical analysis looks at charts and patterns. I use analysis to try to predict price movements. The charts and the patterns give technical analysis clues, about where the price might go. Key Concepts for Beginners 1. Support and Resistance Support = a price level where the stock stops falling (floor). Support works like a floor, for the stock. Support helps the stock stay above that level. Resistance = a price level where the stock stops rising. Resistance acts as a ceiling. 2. Moving Averages 50-day Moving Average (MA) — Short-term trend 200-day Moving Average (MA) — Long-term trend Golden Cross (50-day crosses above 200-day) = Bullish signal Death Cross (50-day crosses below 200-day) = Bearish signal 3. Volume High volume during price increase = Strong buying interest High volume during price decrease = Strong selling pressure Low volume = Weak conviction in the move 4. RSI (Relative Strength Index) I measure whether the stock is overbought or oversold. RSI above 70 = Overbought (might drop) I notice that when the RSI is, below thirty the RSI is oversold and the RSI might rise.
